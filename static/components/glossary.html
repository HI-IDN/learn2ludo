Glossary

Key AI, gameplay, and system terms used throughout the app.

No terms match your search.

A

Agent An AI player that perceives the game state and decides what move to make.
In reinforcement learning, an agent interacts with an environment (the game) by taking actions and receiving rewards. In Learn2Ludo, a bot is an agent that has been trained — or programmed with rules — to play Ludo.

Example: The "Weighted Bot" is an agent that scores each possible move and always picks the best one according to its weights.

B

Blockade Two or more pawns of the same colour stacked on one square, blocking opponents from passing.
A blockade forms when two (or more, depending on the rule set) pawns of the same player share a square. Opponents cannot land on or pass through that square. Blockades are a key defensive and offensive tactic in Ludo.

Example: If your two green pawns sit on square 14, no other player can move through square 14.

Bot (AI player) A computer-controlled player that automatically picks moves.
A bot replaces a human player in a slot and chooses moves without any input. Bots in Learn2Ludo range from purely random players to trained AI models. You can configure bots on the Bots page.

C

Capture Landing on a square occupied by an opponent's pawn and sending it back to their yard.
When a pawn lands on a square that holds an opponent's pawn, the opponent's pawn is captured and returned to their starting yard. The capturing player may receive a bonus roll. Pawns on safe squares cannot be captured.

Example: You roll a 3, your pawn lands on square 22 where an opponent sits — their pawn goes back to start.

Composite dispatching rule (CDR) A decision rule made by combining several simpler dispatching rules with weights.
In scheduling literature, a composite dispatching rule combines multiple priorities into one score. A CDR is a dispatching-rule version of a weighted decision: it uses weights to decide which available job, action, or move should go next. Apollo is Learn2Ludo's CDR example: it combines simple rules such as capture, safety, progress, and activation. If one Apollo weight is set to 100 and the rest to 0, Apollo behaves like that matching simple rule.

Example: Apollo with only the capture weight enabled behaves like Ares.

D

Deterministic decision A decision that always produces the same result given the same inputs — no randomness involved.
A deterministic bot always picks the same move when it faces the same game situation. This makes it predictable and easy to reason about. Contrast with stochastic behaviour, where the same situation can lead to different choices.

Example: A greedy bot that always captures if a capture is available is deterministic.

Dispatching rule A rule that ranks the available actions and dispatches the best one next.
In scheduling literature, a dispatching rule decides which job should be handled next from the jobs currently available. In Learn2Ludo, a dispatching rule decides which legal move a bot should make from the moves currently available.

Example: "Choose a capturing move if one exists" is a dispatching rule.

F

Feature A measurable property of the game state used by an AI to evaluate a move.
Features translate raw game data into numbers that a bot can reason about. Common features in Learn2Ludo include "distance to home", "number of safe pawns", or "threat of being captured". The Weighted Bot combines these features with weights to score each possible move.

Example: "Pawns in home stretch" is a feature with a positive value — more pawns near home is generally better.

G

Game state A complete snapshot of the game at a given moment: all pawn positions, whose turn it is, and the dice roll.
The game state captures everything needed to continue or replay a game. It includes each player's pawn positions, the current turn, any pending dice roll, and the phase (rolling, moving, finished). The AI uses the game state as its input when deciding the next move.

H

Heuristic A rule of thumb or scoring formula used to evaluate how good a move or game position is.
Heuristics give a quick estimate of value without exhaustive calculation. In board games they are often hand-crafted by domain experts. The Weighted Bot in Learn2Ludo is heuristic-based: it assigns scores to moves using a set of weighted features rather than learning from experience.

Example: "Prefer moves that advance a pawn closer to home" is a simple heuristic.

J

Justification (player explanation) A human-readable explanation of why a bot chose a particular move.
When a bot makes a move, the game can display a justification — a short description of which features drove the decision and by how much. This helps players understand what the AI is "thinking" without requiring knowledge of the underlying maths.

M

Model (AI model) The learned parameters that define how a trained bot evaluates positions and selects moves.
After training, an RL bot's knowledge is stored as a model — a set of numbers (weights or a neural network) that encode what it has learned. You can save, load, and compare models on the Bots page.
Move Advancing one pawn by the number shown on the dice.
On each turn a player rolls the dice and must move one eligible pawn the exact number of squares shown. A move can result in landing on a safe square, capturing an opponent, or reaching home. If no pawn can move legally, the turn passes.

P

Pawn One of the four playing pieces each player moves around the board.
Each player controls four pawns that begin in their yard. Pawns must exit the yard (usually by rolling a 6), travel the full track, and reach home to score. A pawn that is captured is returned to the yard.
Preset variant A named collection of rule settings that can be applied to a game in one click.
Preset variants let you quickly switch between different rule configurations — for example "Classic Ludo", "Fast Mode", or a custom research variant — without manually adjusting every individual rule. Presets are managed on the Settings page.

R

Rule set The complete set of rules governing a game session, including movement, captures, and special squares.
The rule set determines how the game is played: what roll is needed to leave the yard, whether blockades are allowed, how safe squares work, and more. Different rule sets can dramatically change game dynamics and bot behaviour.
Round One full pass through the player order, where each active player gets a turn.
A round is complete once every active player has had one turn in the current player order. Extra turns from special rules can make a round contain more actions, but the round concept is still useful for comparing progress across players.

S

Safe square A board position where a pawn cannot be captured by opponents.
Safe squares are marked on the board and provide protection. Any pawn resting on a safe square is immune to capture. The home column (the coloured stretch before home) is always safe. Some rule sets also designate star squares or starting squares as safe.
Simple dispatching rule (SDR) A single-priority rule that ranks the legal moves and picks the best one.
A simple dispatching rule uses one clear priority to choose an action. In Learn2Ludo, bots such as Ares, Athena, Hestia, and Artemis are SDRs: each is a dispatching rule focused on one idea, such as capturing, staying safe, making progress, or activating a pawn.

Example: Hestia is an SDR because it prefers the move that brings a pawn closest to home.

State (game state) Everything the game needs to know at a given moment to continue play.
See Game state. In AI and reinforcement learning, "state" is the general term for the observation an agent receives from its environment before deciding on an action.
Stochastic (random) behaviour Behaviour that involves randomness — the same inputs can lead to different outcomes.
A stochastic bot does not always pick the best move; it samples from a probability distribution over moves. This can make it less predictable and sometimes harder to exploit than a fully deterministic bot. Dice rolls are the core source of stochasticity in Ludo.

Example: A random bot picks any legal move with equal probability — pure stochastic behaviour.

T

Training The process of running many games so an RL bot improves its strategy through experience.
During training, the agent plays thousands of episodes, receives rewards for good outcomes (winning, capturing, advancing), and adjusts its model to maximise future rewards. Training is configured and monitored on the Train page.
Turn One player's opportunity to roll the dice and move a pawn.
Players take turns in a fixed rotation. On each turn the active player rolls the dice, then chooses a pawn to move (if any move is legal). Rolling a 6 may grant an extra turn, depending on the rule set.

W

Weighted bot A bot that scores each move using a weighted sum of game features and picks the highest-scoring move.
The weighted bot assigns a numerical importance (weight) to each feature or dispatching rule. To evaluate a move, it combines those weighted priorities into one score. The move with the highest total score wins. Apollo is the example weighted bot; student-created weighted bots will use sliders to build their own composite dispatching rules.
Weighted decision A decision made by combining multiple factors, each scaled by an importance weight.
Rather than treating all considerations as equal, a weighted decision multiplies each factor by its weight before combining them. Higher-weight factors dominate the final score. A composite dispatching rule is a weighted decision used specifically to choose the next item from the available options.

Example: If "capture opponent" has weight 10 and "advance pawn" has weight 1, the bot will almost always prefer capturing over advancing.
